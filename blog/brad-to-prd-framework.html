From BRAD to PRD: My Framework for Turning Business Requirements into Shipping Products

Every product manager has experienced this: a business stakeholder hands you a document full of requirements, and you need to turn it into something an engineering team can actually build. The gap between a business requirements analysis document (BRAD) and a product requirements document (PRD) is where most miscommunication happens — and where most projects go sideways.

Over the past few years at Logiwa, I've developed a structured framework for bridging that gap. It's not rocket science, but it's repeatable, and it's saved me from shipping the wrong thing more times than I can count.

Why the Gap Exists

Business requirements and product requirements speak different languages. A BRAD says "we need to improve order accuracy." A PRD says "when an operator scans an item that doesn't match the order, display a red warning and prevent the pack from being completed until the correct item is scanned."

The BRAD defines what the business needs. The PRD defines what the product does. The PM's job is to be the translator.

The BRAD-to-PRD Framework

Here's the five-step process I follow for every feature that starts as a business requirement.

Step 1: Decompose the Business Requirement

Most business requirements are actually bundles of smaller requirements. "Improve order accuracy" might include:

  • Scan verification at pack station
  • Real-time inventory discrepancy alerts
  • Quality check workflows for high-value items
  • Reporting dashboards for accuracy metrics

I start by breaking the BRAD into atomic requirements — the smallest units of business value that can be delivered independently. This immediately makes scope manageable and helps with prioritization.

Step 2: Map to User Workflows

For each atomic requirement, I map the end-to-end user workflow. Who triggers the action? What do they see? What decisions do they make? What happens next?

This is where I move from business language to product language. Instead of "improve accuracy," I'm now describing specific screens, interactions, and data flows.

Step 3: Define the Acceptance Criteria

Each workflow step gets acceptance criteria written in Given-When-Then format:

  • Given an operator is at the pack station with order #1234 open
  • When they scan an item barcode that doesn't match any line item in the order
  • Then the system displays a red "Item Mismatch" warning, plays an audible alert, and prevents the pack-complete action until the correct item is scanned or a supervisor override is entered

This level of specificity is what engineering needs. It's also where you catch the edge cases — what happens if the barcode is unreadable? What if the item is a substitute? What if the order was modified after picking started?

Step 4: Trace Back to the Business Requirement

This is the step most PMs skip, and it's the most important one for stakeholder trust. Every acceptance criterion in your PRD should trace back to a specific business requirement in the BRAD.

I use a simple traceability matrix — a table that maps each BRAD requirement to the corresponding PRD sections, user stories, and acceptance criteria. When a stakeholder asks "did you include the thing I asked for?" I can point to exactly where it lives in the spec.

Traceability isn't bureaucracy — it's insurance. It protects you when scope creep tries to sneak in, and it gives stakeholders confidence that their needs were heard.

Step 5: Validate with Both Sides

Before the PRD is "done," I run two validation passes:

  1. Business validation — Walk the stakeholder through the PRD and confirm that every business requirement is addressed. This isn't a rubber stamp; it's a real review where they can flag gaps.
  2. Technical validation — Walk engineering through the PRD and confirm feasibility. This is where you learn that the "simple" barcode scan actually requires a new hardware integration, or that the real-time alert needs a WebSocket connection that doesn't exist yet.

These reviews almost always surface issues. Better to find them in a document review than in a sprint demo.

Tools That Help

A few tools and practices that make this framework smoother:

  • Jira Product Discovery — for capturing and prioritizing the atomic requirements from Step 1
  • Claude / AI assistants — for generating first-draft acceptance criteria and edge cases (see my post on AI-powered PRDs)
  • Traceability tables — even a simple spreadsheet mapping BRAD items to PRD sections
  • Workflow diagrams — for complex multi-step processes, a visual flow catches gaps that prose misses

The Payoff

When you consistently follow a framework like this, three things happen:

  1. Engineering trusts your specs — because they're specific, testable, and complete. Fewer mid-sprint surprises.
  2. Stakeholders trust the process — because they can see their requirements reflected in the product plan, with traceability.
  3. You ship the right thing — because you've validated from both the business and technical side before writing a line of code.

The BRAD-to-PRD gap is where products succeed or fail. Having a repeatable framework for crossing it is one of the most valuable tools in a product manager's toolkit.
